12 Angry Men (1957)

Sidney Lumet
96 minutes
Crime, Drama

English
Rating:
5.0 / 5.0
★★★★★
★★★★★

12 Angry Men is a timeless legal film that bears out the importance of two things: the purpose of deliberation and the scope of reasonable doubt.

We open with a shot of the courthouse, tilting up to show the building's size, mirroring the enormity of the task put before the jurors. They have heard a case of murder and now the judge reminds them of their role and the potential severity of their verdict, saying, "The bench will not entertain a recommendation for mercy. The death sentence is mandatory in this case. You're faced with a grave responsibility.”

The deliberation room starts hot and only grows hotter as the jurors greet one another and discussion gets underway. They are only known by their number and shared details. #7 is eager to wrap things up, with Yankees tickets burning a hole in his pocket; #3 is a tough guy who runs a messenger service and is certain of the defendant's guilt. Identifying information, other than names, is accumulated about all of them as they deliberate.

The film explores the virtues of the various methods by which the jurors vote, each serving a different narrative purpose. The first round is open so that the lone disputant of Juror #8, played by Henry Fonda, can be identified.

#8 systematically tears the case apart. He recontextualizes the defendant’s background and questions it as a reasonable motive. Defense counsel is dismissed as lazy, incompetent or disincentivized. Additionally, the witness testimony seems unreliable on closer examination. #8 is even able to produce an exact copy of the supposedly unique murder weapon. He is giving them reason after reason to doubt.

The second round of voting excludes Juror #8, who says he will abide by a guilty verdict if the others remain in agreement. This time they initiate the vote as a secret ballot.

This causes two jurors (#9 and then #5) to reconsider, both proceeding to offer other reasons the defendant may not necessarily be guilty. As the debate is ongoing, frequent close-ups of our characters are used, sometimes to give us the impression they are looking directly at us and including us in the process.

The weather changes to rain just as the voting conditions have shifted from the starting count of 11-1 to the new count of 6-6.

Our main holdouts come in the form of a loud and proud racist who hurls his bigotry at the defendant (#10), a know-it-all stockbroker (#4), and an enraged and estranged father (#3). The racist goes on a tirade so shameful, everyone in the room telegraphs condemnation before he is silenced. The know-it-all is neutralized through being irrefutably proven wrong about his confidence in recollection and witness testimony.

The enraged father, taking his own family woes out on the defendant, proves the most difficult to move. Defiant, he reaches for his notes, but instead a picture of his alienated son spills out with them, and he says, "That's it, that's the whole case." Slowly breaking down with the realization of what he is doing, #3 buries his head and changes his vote to "not guilty", bringing the count to 0-12.

Deliberation brought about a closer look at not only the defendant, but the jurors themselves; this proves key in revealing how their own motivations shape the outcome of the case. The jurors, mostly eager to be done with their task, find that they accidentally have done an effective job: they establish whether or not there is cause for reasonable doubt. #8 starts as the lone stone in the road; as the others join, a wall is built. Eventually, the road is erased from the map altogether as the road to a guilty verdict is deemed too treacherous and uncertain.

All that is required for justice to prevail is for 12 Angry Men to get in a room and aggressively “separate the facts from the fancy,” both in the case and in themselves.
