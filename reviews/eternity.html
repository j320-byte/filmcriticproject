Eternity (2025)

David Freyne
114 minutes
Comedy, Romance

English
Rating:
2.0 / 5.0
★★★★★
★★★★★

As a romance film, Eternity is odd; the premise actually results in surprisingly minimal on-screen romance, and the movie has a peculiar understanding of couples. For many who are already in long-term, loving relationships, the premise may seem like a misfire from the start, resembling more of a romantic wedge than any sort of ardor.

The film's vision of Eternity is tired. Variations of a waystation/temporal nexus are not an underexplored concept. The film even "borrows heavily" from the aesthetic of other renditions.

The film seems to purposefully avoid making any actual statement or postulation about the afterlife, other than to perhaps say that if there is an afterlife, it's probably just as dysfunctional and flawed as life on Earth. It strikes the reviewer as a sort of pointless cynicism.

As a particular strength, the performances are anywhere from good to great. Our key trio perform well, though apart from them, Da'Vine Joy Randolph must be acknowledged for her consistently funny delivery and tone.

Miles Teller and Callum Turner, as Larry and Luke respectively, are at their best when depicted bonding over their shared love for Joan, played by Elizabeth Olsen. Larry's knowledge of Luke and Luke's ignorance of events after his demise both aid in the effectiveness of this exchange. “This one is for Korea! Not enough films made about it... Well, except MASH,” Larry proclaims, notably mixing film and television, before Luke, a Korean vet who died at war, responds, “What the hell's MASH?” Larry exclaims, “Ah, man. You gotta watch MAS*H!”

The reviewer observes that while the film preaches inclusivity, our main trio are uniform in a number of characteristics. There is nothing strictly wrong with either of these things individually, but when put together, it does feel like talking the talk and not walking the walk.

The film can be read as a female-centric drama as much as romance. However, at times the film goes beyond female-centricity and feels anti-male in attitude. One of the eternities being offered is "Man Free World", which is full, with another coming soon. More generally, the husbands are mostly depicted as obstacles, not contributors, to Joan's happiness.

The film makes sure to include a sequence where both men are rejected equally and independence is asserted by Joan. Larry is seen falling on the sword, giving his lifelong partner the green light to leave him for what amounts to a fling; the level to which Larry degrades himself to make Joan happy almost has a streak of cuckoldry. Luke does the same, establishing that whatever Joan wants, she should have, and the men should derive pleasure from that. Joan's agency is not simply put up on a pedestal; it's worshipped as a god.

While representation is to be applauded, Eternity mishandles its LGBT elements through heavy-handedness. Luke, despite being presented as completely straight, is mandated to have a couple of past sexual encounters with men. "Bromance" world is at one point prominently displayed. At one point a friend of Joan excessively references her lesbianism with specificity, so much so that it simply becomes strange. While one of the coordinators, Ryan, is not explicitly gay, his manner with Joan is very "gay best friend" coded. The two coordinators, later seen as a couple, are heard discussing the possibility of polyamory at the end. None of these additions were organic or necessary. It is almost as if the film wanted to be one thing but was only allowed to be another.

The conclusion offers resolution for our characters, but persists in its inability to describe or address the state of the afterlife. While mystery as a part of the afterlife is a strength, this film made a point of calling out a high level of dysfunction. Yet, as the story ends, we treat the characters as if they have terminated at an acceptable status quo.

Despite getting some things right in the end, it turns out Eternity is a bit bleak.
