High Noon (1952)

Fred Zinnemann
85 minutes
Western

English
Rating:
4.5 / 5.0
★★★★★
★★★★★

The audience feels every moment tick by in High Noon, as time is as much the enemy as the villains themselves.

The commentary on human nature is fairly cynical, with Kane, Gary Cooper's character, particularly disaffected at the end of the film as he, victorious, throws his badge aside. The film is also a good reminder that action is no substitute for drama; the meat of the film is watching Kane try, and fail, to convince members of the population to help him.

The best depiction of Kane’s efforts is in the church scene where we see a complex display of cowardice. They assign blame, to the Northern politicians, to Kane himself. Various grievances are aired, as the civilians feel twice taxed having paid Kane and now being asked to aid in the towns defense themselves. We even make time for an “I told ya so” and one moron who still doesn’t believe anything is really happening. But the gut punch comes when a character, played by Thomas Mitchell, declares that for the greater good Kane must retreat, telling him, “It’s better for you and it’s better for us”.

Powerful visual storytelling is present as well, such as the close-up of the Miller brothers inspecting their firearms and the clock marking the passing seconds as Kane sits down to write his will.

The film also sees an impressive character in Helen Ramírez, portrayed by Katy Jurado, an independent figure who encourages Grace Kelly's character to support and aid her husband in his plight, rather than run in fear; significant for the time, she was played by a race-appropriate actress whose role was far from minor.

This film is a striking testament to the amount of ground that can be covered with less than ninety minutes of film. In High Noon, storytelling and tension are also at their peak, making it not just a solid Western, but an excellent film.
