Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975)

Terry Gilliam, Terry Jones
91 minutes
Comedy, Fantasy

English
Rating:
4.0 / 5.0
★★★★★
★★★★★

Monty Python and the Holy Grail ultimately functions as sketch comedy connected by a loose narrative. But within that light format, satire abounds.

The comedy is hilariously idiosyncratic, starting with the opening credits and a supposed subtitle dispute. The jokes are varied and constant. Throughout the film, coconuts are used to replace the hoofbeats of horses. At one point, a black-clad knight allows himself to be dismembered rather than admit defeat, haughty even as a stump. The comedy also becomes quite meta; one of the knights manages to kill a modern-day historian which provokes a police response.

The cast of characters is ridiculous without exception. To the players’ credit, many held multiple roles, a tradition in keeping with sketch comedy. It should be noted that every player in every role they took up performed admirably.

Perhaps the most intriguing part of the film is the way in which it satirizes Arthurian lore. The film, with whimsy, attacks and makes a mockery of the prevailing conception of Arthurian lore present in popular culture. Instead, it seeks to replace it with something less fantastic and far funnier.

This replacement is seen in such scenes as when the commoners are collecting mud; they are not noble and loyal subjects. Life is grinding them to the bone and they don't even know who their king is. Much to King Arthur's dismay, the one commoner versed in politics is in favor of overthrowing the monarchy. Despite being revered in modern renditions of his story, this King Arthur is constantly met with disrespect and disdain. In another scene, Lancelot is seen violently cutting through peasants with abandon; the film’s version of a knight seems perfectly prepared to engage in violence without purpose.

Demanding a certain comedic sensibility but rewarding it greatly, Monty Python and the Holy Grail has only one thing to say to Arthurian lore: "I didn't know we had a king. I thought we were an autonomous collective."
