Spider-Man (2002)

Sam Raimi
121 minutes
Action, Superhero

English
Rating:
4.5 / 5.0
★★★★★
★★★★★

Spider-Man represents an expertly coordinated collision between comic books and film, brought about through careful characterization, creative camerawork and use of color, and the conjuration of elements that were once only imagined.

Characters are expertly brought to life from the page, from the dorky Peter Parker/Spider-Man, played by Tobey Maguire, to the profoundly wise Uncle Ben, played by Cliff Robertson. Maguire shines throughout the film while Robertson finds his strongest moments come when giving sage advice to Peter and later when Uncle Ben lays dying. Willem Dafoe is convincingly hammy as Norman Osborn/Green Goblin, with a sharp tongue and maniacal laughter to match. The sequence of Norman and the Goblin having a back-and-forth through the mirror showcases Dafoe's mastery over body language and facial expression.

Modest modifications are made to our super characters, but only to facilitate their transition to screen. Spider-Man has shed his crafted web-shooters in favor of organic ones and The Green Goblin has been technologized to match the efforts of the film version of Norman Osborn.

Visually, the film seeks inspiration from the source medium rather than pursuing a wholesale reinvention. Colors are often highly saturated, with red especially being accentuated so as to pop on screen. Shots are framed like comic panels in motion, particularly those of Spider-Man swinging which perfectly recreate the posture seen in the source. The suit designs are excellent, with Spider-Man getting one of his best looks yet. The webbing on his suit is given its own material layer and the eyes have been made more mirrorlike, occasionally reflecting the cityscape vividly.

Through adaptation to film, the story is afforded certain creative resources it was denied in print format. For example, Spider-Man's use of his web is accompanied by a satisfying "thwip" sound, giving sonic identity to our hero's powers that the comics never could. Another example is the fight sequences which, while previously broken into smaller sets of images, are now rendered fluidly. In print, the action occurs at the reader's pace; in the movie, we can watch Spider-Man and the Goblin trade blows in real-time.

And finally, true to the comics, the movie ends on the perfect note of leaving the audience wanting more; they're clamoring for the "next issue". Spider-Man doesn't just act as an origin story or its own superhero outing; it spun its webbed world from its source material and fixed it in place for subsequent entries. Multiple things beg for follow-up: Harry's desire for revenge, Mary Jane's curiosity surrounding Spider-Man, and the massive burden of duty put on Peter.

A film that watches the way a stack of comic books reads, Spider-Man retains an exceptional amount of strength from its source and is further empowered by the unique capabilities of its own medium. And as Uncle Ben said: "With great power comes great responsibility." In its responsibility as an adaptation, Spider-Man is respectful, inventive, and page-perfect in its capture of the material.
