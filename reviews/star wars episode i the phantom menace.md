*Star Wars: Episode I - The Phantom Menace* was significantly burdened from the outset. It had to capitalize on new technology to further the Star Wars mythos, relaunch the series after a hiatus, and lay the groundwork for future films.

The advent of CGI gave Lucas a whole new avenue of visual exploration and the results range all the way from good to poor. Some of the new settings and aliens are eye-catching but others only serve as gaudy digital additions to a once deliberately practical world. A particular fight late in the movie even takes place with a Windows XP desktop wallpaper as the backdrop. The film's visual identity was a point of controversy for fans. Still, entire sequences of *The Phantom Menace* would have been impossible without digital effects. And while it is easy to point to the hokey battle droids and gungans, it ignores real visual strengths elsewhere. This is ironic because while some of the film's visual strength comes from digital effects, much of it remains grounded in practicality.

As examples, the podracing sequence is an absolutely incredible display of the film's ability to use digital effects deftly and in a sustained manner. In terms of practicality, everything from the prop blasters, to lightsabers, to the make-up and costuming work on Queen Amidala and Darth Maul is excellent; the film may no longer be decidedly practical, but it did not discard the skillset altogether.

Receiving respectable marks on the technical side, the question was how audiences would respond. The series had not seen a new entry in 16 years. Expectations that the film would maintain fidelity with the originals while also achieving modernity were incredibly high. But more important than the conversation surrounding the film: could *The Phantom Menace* successfully redeploy the Star Wars formula?

If there is a fault, it is following the formula too closely, not departing from it. We spend the early parts of the film on an arid planet (the same one we've been to before, in fact) where a young male with incredible power is hidden away. He must then make the daring journey into space where he plays a key part in a military engagement, guided by his connection with the force.

Also staying true to the originals, a legendary lightsaber battle is delivered with the orchestrally and otherwise sonically incredible "Duel of the Fates" accompanying it. While the placement of this battle is classic, the choreography is all new. We see a three-party conflict, Obi-Wan fighting like an eager apprentice, while Qui-Gon fights with the patience and discipline of a weathered Jedi, and best of all, Maul swings, jumps and taunts wildly with an aggression only found in the Sith. The reveal of the dual lightsaber to match his two opponents is visual ecstasy.

In laying the groundwork for future films, *The Phantom Menace* is fascinating in its departure from the first entry in the last trilogy. The departure is of course that *The Phantom Menace* ever concerned itself with sequels in the first place. While inconsistent with the original formula, this stands as a legitimate improvement. The film doesn't end on a production note of "maybe, we'll see what happens", it is custom built for later addition. And given the episodic nature the series took on, this is fitting.

While it doesn't reach the heights of its predecessors, the film still managed induction into the cultural lexicon, serving as fodder for nostalgia and memes to this day. From technology, to maintaining the brand, and preparation for the future, *The Phantom Menace* is an all around success.
