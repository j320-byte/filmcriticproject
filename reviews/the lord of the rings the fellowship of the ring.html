The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring (2001)

Peter Jackson
228 minutes
Adventure, Fantasy

English

★★★★★

The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring is a superlative work in film, showcasing mastery over the screen, the source, and the audience's attention.

Absolutely supermassive in scope, every component of the film manages to deeply impress whether it's cinematography, set design, or story.

Jackson’s knowledge of Tolkien's Middle-earth is dizzyingly comprehensive, shown in his abiding respect for the source material. Still, Jackson shows wisdom in knowing when to commit to story departures in order to adapt the material to film. Sometimes omissions seem to be made on the basis of overcomplication, preoccupation with historical context, and events that would only serve to exhaust the film’s runtime. These are good reasons for omission.

The film is an ocular feast. Cinematography is highly varied, offering the viewer many vantage points from which to look into Middle-earth. Color and location are synonymous, acting as an important indicator for our audience as we move through our journey. The landscapes are inspirational of wonder and wanderlust, the beauty of New Zealand on full display.

Costumes are impossibly authentic, helping create the visual identity of Middle-earth. Armor appears battle ready, and appropriately brutish or ornate as necessary. The starring weapons, such as Gandalf’s staves, Frodo's Sting and Aragorn's Andúril, strongly distinguish themselves from the rest of the prop weapons.

The score of the film is genre-defining, never once missing a note, musical, dramatic, or otherwise.

But where all things are concerned, the film shines most when Gandalf faces down the Balrog. His rebuke of the demon is powerful and the words "you shall not pass" have been carved into the cultural consciousness. Cinematography, music, and story intensity all align to imbue this scene with the power of a nuclear reaction.

Visually spectacular, narratively satisfying, and deeply immersive, there cannot be found a finer first entry to a film series than The Fellowship of the Ring.
